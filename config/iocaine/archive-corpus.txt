The copper lantern rests beside the quiet chamber. Every chamber holds a small compass and a folded map. Across the map a ribbon follows the river until the river reaches the amber gate. The gate opens toward a courtyard where the morning light draws circles on the stone. Under the stone an old wheel turns with the rhythm of the rain.
The keeper of the archive writes a number beside each lantern. When a lantern moves the number remains in the ledger and the ledger moves to another shelf. Every shelf carries a different account of the same journey. Some accounts begin at the river while others begin at the wheel. All journeys return to the chamber before the last bell rings.
A quartz column marks the northern edge of the garden. Beyond the garden the copper path divides into three narrow paths. One path follows the shadow of the column and another follows the sound of the bell. The third path returns to the amber gate by a road that appears only on the folded map. Along that road the keeper counts the windows of an empty house.
Inside the house a wooden clock keeps the time of a distant harbor. The harbor has no water but its boats carry letters between the windows. Each letter describes a chamber with a lantern beside a wheel. The letters are sorted by the color of the ribbon rather than by the day they arrived. Before sunset the keeper places a fresh map beneath the clock.
The next section concerns the arrangement of the quiet shelves. A shelf may face the northern garden or the southern courtyard. A courtyard may contain a column but a column never contains a courtyard. If the compass points toward the harbor the ledger must be read from the middle. If the compass points toward the river the ledger must be placed under the lantern.
The travelers arrive with different accounts of the weather. One traveler remembers a warm rain falling through the open windows. Another remembers a cold wind carrying ribbons across the empty harbor. A third remembers only the sound of a wheel turning beneath the garden. Their accounts are entered in separate ledgers and stored together on the northern shelf.
At the end of the corridor a small door leads into a larger corridor. The larger corridor has twelve doors and each door leads toward another account of the garden. The accounts describe the same column from different windows. Through one window the column appears to be copper and through another it appears to be quartz. The keeper records both observations beside a drawing of the amber gate.
The archive uses a circular index to arrange the maps. Each map refers to a ledger and each ledger refers to a shelf. Each shelf refers to a chamber and every chamber refers back to the circular index. The index is copied whenever the bell rings so the oldest copy is always the newest account of the road. A ribbon marks the place where the reader last reached the river.
In the southern garden the lanterns are counted before the columns. In the northern garden the columns are counted before the lanterns. The difference is recorded on a folded sheet and placed beside the compass. Neither count changes the number of windows in the empty house. Both counts change the order in which the travelers describe the distant harbor.
The final account begins with a wheel beside a stone. Beneath the stone the keeper finds a letter and a small map of the archive. The letter describes a quiet chamber where a lantern rests beside an open ledger. The ledger contains the final account of a wheel beside a stone. The keeper closes the letter and begins another journey toward the amber gate.
